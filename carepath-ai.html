CarePath AI

An AI front office for a one-person patient transport business.

Executive Summary

The driver cannot answer the phone

Non-emergency patient transport in the West Midlands is a single-operator business: one person driving, who cannot answer the phone while at the wheel. Every missed call is a booking lost to whoever picks up first.

CarePath answers the phone, the email and the chat. It quotes from a real coverage and pricing model, books the journey, sends the confirmation, puts it in the diary, and escalates to the owner only when a human genuinely needs to decide.

The owner runs the whole business from a phone call or a Telegram thread — asking what happened today, chasing a customer, having it ring someone back on their behalf.

Design Decision

The safety gate is code, not a prompt

This is the decision I would defend first. The gate runs before any model call, and it can only raise risk — never lower it.

So “never quote a price for an ad-hoc journey” is a property of the system, not a hope about how a model behaves when a caller pushes.

01

Gate runs first

Risk is assessed in code before any model call, so the safety decision never depends on the model’s cooperation.

02

One-way ratchet

The gate can only raise risk, never lower it. Nothing downstream — including the model — can talk the system into being less careful than the gate decided.

03

Retrieval with a measured floor

Calibrated over 22 queries: legitimate questions scored 0.61–0.86, irrelevant ones 0.53–0.56. The floor sits at 0.59, in the gap between them.

04

Fix the corpus, not the threshold

When a genuine question fell below the floor, the fix was to add the missing document — never to lower the floor.

The Hardest Problem

Honesty, not capability

An agent that claims it sent an email it didn’t send is worse than one that fails loudly, because it costs the owner a check they didn’t know they needed.

Most of the engineering went into making the system’s account of its own work trustworthy in both directions.

It must not claim work it didn’t do

A false report of success is the expensive failure — it removes the owner’s reason to check, and the error surfaces later as a lost customer rather than an alert.

It must not be punished for reporting a real limitation

The other half of the problem, and the easier one to get wrong: a system tuned only against overclaiming learns to hedge everything. Accurately reporting a genuine limit has to be a safe thing to do.

670 tests, all running offline

The suite runs with no network and no model calls, so the behaviour above is verified deterministically rather than sampled from a live system.

What This Demonstrates

Safety as a system property

Constraints enforced in code, not prompts

A rule that matters is written where a persuasive caller cannot reach it. The model is asked to be useful; it is not asked to be the last line of defence.

Thresholds earned from measurement

The confidence floor sits in an observed gap between legitimate and irrelevant queries — a number derived from data, then held to when it became inconvenient.

Self-reporting treated as an engineering problem

Trustworthiness in both directions — neither overclaiming nor over-hedging — designed for deliberately and verified offline.
